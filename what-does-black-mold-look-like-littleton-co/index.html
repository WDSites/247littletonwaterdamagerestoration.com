What Does Black Mold Look Like in Littleton, CO?

Dark spots on a wall or ceiling are not always mold, and color alone cannot settle it. Here is how to tell what you may be looking at and what to do next.

By the 24/7 Littleton Water Damage Restoration team. Updated 2026.

What does black mold look like to the eye?

People usually describe it as dark green, black or deep gray spotting, sometimes fuzzy, sometimes flat and smudged. It often appears in patches that grow outward from a damp area, with a fringe that looks uneven rather than neat.

That description fits a lot of other things too, which is why this question has no one-look answer. The honest answer to what black mold looks like is: sometimes like this, sometimes like dirt, and often only a lab or a trained inspector can say.

Things that look like mold but are not

Plenty of dark marks on walls and ceilings are not growth at all. Soot, dust and cobwebs collect along cold edges. Ghosting is a dark outline that forms where framing sits behind drywall and the surface runs colder. Water stains, rust bleed from nails and cooking residue can all look convincing from across the room.

The clues in the third column are hints, not a diagnosis. A wipe test can be misleading, because mold can also wipe away and leave stained material behind.

Where it hides

Mold favors places that stay damp and still: behind furniture pushed against an exterior wall, under sinks, around window frames, inside closets, on the back of drywall in a bathroom, and in crawl spaces. Basement corners with poor air movement are common spots. Attic sheathing can show growth when warm indoor air condenses against a cold roof deck.

Look for the combination of a dark patch and a moisture source. Without a water source nearby, a dark mark is more likely dirt or staining.

Why color alone cannot identify it

Mold comes in many colors, including white, green, gray, brown, orange and black. The popular label black mold covers a color description, not one fixed identity, and plenty of molds look dark. Color can change with the surface, the age of growth and the lighting.

No honest inspector names a type from a photo. Identification takes sampling and lab work, and even then, the scope of remediation depends on moisture and how much material is affected, not on a name.

No health claims here

We cannot tell you what a dark patch means for anyone's health. Mold needs moisture, remediation and testing follow ANSI/IICRC S520, and questions about symptoms belong to a physician.

Testing options

Options range from a visual inspection with moisture readings to surface tape lifts and air samples sent to a lab. Each answers a narrow question, and none is a complete answer alone. Air samples vary by day and conditions, so a result is a snapshot rather than a verdict.

A thoughtful inspector begins with moisture mapping, because finding the wet material often explains more than a lab report. Whether you test, ask who performs the sampling, and what standard they follow.

  • Visual inspection with a moisture meter and thermal camera
  • Tape or swab surface samples for lab review
  • Air sampling compared with an outdoor sample
  • Post-remediation clearance by an independent party

What to do while you decide

Keep the area dry and ventilated, fix any obvious leak, and avoid scrubbing or sanding the patch. Disturbing it can spread material to clean rooms. Take dated photos so you can see whether it changes.

Reading the room around the spot

Context tells you more than the patch itself. Check whether the wall is cold to the touch, whether a pipe, window or roof edge is nearby, and whether the spot gets worse after showers or storms. A patch that stays the same for months and sits away from any water is less likely to be active growth.

Write down what you find and bring it to any inspector. The history of the room is often the fastest route to the cause.
