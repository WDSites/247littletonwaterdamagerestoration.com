How the pages differ

Each town has its own page because the water problems differ. Englewood is an older inner-ring city with its own utilities, and its page covers sewer and drainage contacts. Denver is covered by neighborhood on its south and southwest side, where mid-century brick homes dominate. Lone Tree is newer, with finished basements and stormwater projects.

Aurora is covered through its creek flood record and its many subdivisions, and Morrison through its century-long history of flash floods on Bear Creek. Reading the page for your town tells you what we have seen and what to watch for.

Distances are rough road estimates from our Littleton office, and we never promise an arrival time. Call and give us the street address, and we will tell you what we can do and when.

Why local detail matters for water damage

Homes in these towns were built in different decades, with different foundations, drainage and utilities. A 1950s brick ranch in south Denver has different weak points than a 2000s house in Lone Tree, and a foothills home near Bear Creek faces different flood exposure than a subdivision in Aurora. Knowing the housing stock helps us ask better questions on the phone and look in the right places on site.

Water also reaches a home from different directions: a failed supply line or water heater inside, a sewer backup from the street, or surface runoff and groundwater from outside. Each has a different cleanup path. Sewer and flood water are treated as contaminated, so porous materials come out instead of being dried in place.

Before you call

If it is safe, close the main water valve, switch off power to the wet area at the breaker only if you can reach it without standing in water, and move valuables out of the wet area. Do not run a household vacuum on water. If you smell gas or see sparks, leave and call 911 first.

The list of towns is deliberately short. We would rather cover a few areas well than list every suburb and answer none of them properly.

Common situations across these towns

  • Finished basements with wet carpet pad and insulation after a supply line, sump or water heater failure.
  • Sewer backups that bring contaminated water through a floor drain.
  • Spring snowmelt and summer storm runoff finding its way through window wells and foundation cracks.
  • Winter frozen-pipe bursts in crawl spaces, garages and exterior walls.
  • Slow leaks behind walls and under sinks that show up as stains, odor or soft flooring.

Each of these has a different first step, which is why the town pages and the service pages are written separately. If you are unsure which applies, the number is the same for every town and a dispatcher will walk you through it.

For general moisture and mold background, the US EPA publishes plain guidance on cleaning up after water damage, and Colorado public health agencies publish guidance on mold. Both are worth reading before you decide what to handle yourself.
