Spring and summer storms: Aurora's flood record

The USGS reports that during the September 2013 flood about 15 inches of rain fell in Aurora, and that flooding in the Denver area was largely contained to select streams in Aurora and the metro. Toll Gate Creek peaked at 5,470 cubic feet per second. Sand Creek near its mouth peaked at 14,900, the highest since at least 1992 but below its 1957 peak of 25,500.

That is the main Aurora fact: a very large city where specific creeks carry the risk. If you live near Sand Creek or Toll Gate Creek, check the FEMA flood map for your address and ask your insurer whether flood coverage applies, because most homeowners policies exclude it.

A city built in decades: what that means for your house

Aurora grew from 74,974 residents in 1970 to 158,588 in 1980, and by 2020 it counted 386,261. That kind of growth means hundreds of subdivisions built in different eras, with different plumbing, roofing and basement designs. A house from the 1970s boom and a house from the 2000s have different failure patterns.

Older homes tend to have original supply lines, water heaters at the end of their service life and older roofs. Newer homes more often fail at appliance connections and finished basements. We map moisture before we choose a plan.

Aurora is also unusual in that it lies in Arapahoe, Adams and Douglas counties, so building and permit contacts vary by address. Ask the city's building division which rules apply to your rebuild, because we do not guess at code.

Hail and wind are a regular part of the metro summer, and Aurora's big roofs take the brunt. A shingle torn off at night can let water in for hours before anyone notices. The telltale signs are a brown ceiling spot, a drip in the attic or insulation that feels cold and wet. Photograph the roof side and the ceiling side before anything is moved, and call so we can protect the room and begin drying.
