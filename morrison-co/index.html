Bear Creek and a long flood record

Morrison is a small foothills town in Jefferson County, and its relationship with water is documented. The Mile High Flood District lists 24 floods on Bear Creek since the 1860s, with 45 deaths. The July 1896 flood on Cub Creek killed 27 and remains the largest on record. A cloudburst near Idledale on July 7, 1933, killed five and sent about 8,110 cubic feet per second through Morrison.

In September 1938, six people drowned between Morrison and Kittredge and damage reached 450,000 dollars. The most recent significant event the district lists was on May 6, 1973, at 1,480 cubic feet per second in Morrison.

Water damage restoration in Morrison, CO means being honest about that history. Most calls today are not creek floods. They are runoff reaching a basement door, a failed supply line, or a roof leak. Still, anyone who lives near Bear Creek, Mount Vernon Creek or a drainage gulch should check the FEMA flood map for their address and ask their insurer about flood coverage.

Freeze and snowmelt in the foothills

Winter brings its own problem. Foothills pipes in crawl spaces and exterior walls freeze, and a cracked line can run for hours before anyone is home to notice. If you leave a cabin or second home empty in cold weather, a neighbor with a key and the shut-off location is worth more than any equipment. When you find the water, shut the main, call the 24/7 line and tell us whether the house is occupied.

Snowmelt is the other seasonal visitor. Meltwater against a foundation wall finds a window well or a crack long before it reaches a drain, and a basement can take on water with no rain in the forecast.

The historic town and the places around it

Morrison's historic district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1976, and the town has old stone and frame buildings. Red Rocks Park and Amphitheatre and Dinosaur Ridge are near Morrison and bring visitors and traffic, so the roads can be crowded on event nights.

Older buildings carry older plumbing and roofs. A leak in a historic structure needs careful drying and restoration, and we explain what we cut and why before work starts.
